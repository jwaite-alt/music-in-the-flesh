## **A system of the musical world**

The humanistic aspiration to provide an education in the best that has been thought and said (and painted, played, sung and made) has always had its work cut out in a prevailing political economy that valorises exceptionalism and accumulation at the cost of distributed advantage. Generational attempts to adapt or at least curb the worst excesses of a rigged system have met with success sufficient for survival so far, but citadels for storming and other traditional approaches to meaningful change are well guarded. Meanwhile, everyday exigencies demand and absorb the most committed effort in pursuit of urgent but tactical respite: the next budget, the next timetable…

In this environment, humanistic education is readily parsed as a luxury. Its regressive reassignment to a privileged cohort appears inescapable, and indeed arguable as the natural order of things. All else is subject to streamlining and the efficient administration of measurement to meet criteria required by the ‘world of work’.

In much the same way as fundamental research in the empirical sciences, a humanistic education goes in search of questions as yet unasked, seeking complication so as to complicate the minds of learners in the best sense – enriching relationships between ideas and the people who share them in both dispute and collaborative development. Beyond this, there is no pre-planned objective, no learning outcome or performance indicator. There are results of course, but they can’t be anticipated.

So far, so anarchic utopia. But what if instead of the pursuit of top-down system-wide change, we sought bottom-up, narrowly selective change, and focused on something undervalued and underserved by the mainstream with a view to an exemplary shake-up of something already shaky? The proposition is that a modest, well-made exemplar can do what argument alone cannot: show that something is possible, offer others a working model to copy or contest, and loosen the system at the joints where it is already weakest.

And so a field in which to play out these ideas may be music education, with a centre of gravity in the genus known as ‘classical’ – a label ripe for retirement and reinvention, but one that can be understood to embrace all music with the sincerity of intent to enrich, deepen and complicate the inner lives, bodily sensations and relationships of those who perceive it. In our project, based on this premise and building on work by Bettina Varwig and others, we aim to relocate this music from the friction-free mental space into which it has been driven, back to the whole bodies of listeners and performers alike, and the shifting spaces between them.

More widely, then, three key spheres of activity in which classical music touches lives – education, the profession and the amateur world – are often kept distinct, with less traffic between them than there should be. Contact exists, but it tends to be a visit rather than shared membership, and the routes by which people move from one sphere to another are narrowly defined and heavily regulated. While this presents a tough nut to crack, the points at which we might make inroads are many.

**Music education** in England is under-resourced and unevenly spread, both in the difficulty of finding teachers to stay the course and in the lack of emphasis put on music in the overall subject mix (1). For many thus processed, music ultimately falls into the category of fast-moving consumer goods, shaped by the same forces that manage toothpaste and toilet roll. A minority, skewed by income (2), take specialist instrumental lessons. In many ways their musical fate is even more of a missed opportunity. The orientation of instrumental teaching towards the development of virtuosic performance (as opposed to music making for life) is [well explored here](https://ecumene.substack.com/p/what-are-we-teaching-music-for), and by that analysis sows the seeds of failure. Some do, however, go on to a musical speciality in higher education. Of these, a winnowing ‘back’ to education is common (often, it seems, a fallback in response to no progress in professional music, or a filler while waiting for it). They are outnumbered, though, by those who go on to do work in which music plays no part.

**Professional music making** is of course permeable to great talent, but the nurture and cost of such talent generally fall outside the cultural and financial reach of those without an inherited investment in music. For those who graduate to it, conditions may no longer (or no longer entirely) follow the tradition of _[Whiplash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whiplash_\(2014_film\))_ or ‘[the screaming skull](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georg_Solti)’, but extreme competition and [temperament](https://slippedisc.com/2023/08/exclusive-sir-john-eliot-gardiner-quits-trojans-after-singer-incident/) take their toll in a market economy where revenue streams are increasingly reliant on ageing and socially narrow audiences for performance. If not entirely a closed shop, the profession keeps entry narrow and rewards the idea that talent is scarce. While innovation does happen, and, in specific environments, demand and attendance are high, the general picture is one of decline. The reinforcement of formulaic, well-worked models of performance attempts to shore up turnout through familiarity and to fit efficiently with the modalities and conventions of presentation as they are encoded in the layout of venues and the entrenched etiquette of attendance. The disembodiment of music in both performance and listening is a consequence of this, and a further ingredient of irrelevance. Music in the Flesh’s historical research shows the contrast: early modern writers described music acting on open, porous bodies, where the modern concert asks listeners to sit still and silent.

It is easy to glamorise a lost tradition of **amateur music making**. What the non-existence of recording technology enabled was not a universal knees-up in parlours across the world. Material and cultural privilege clearly afforded a degree of access to the ‘higher’ forms of musical engagement, but echoes of pianoside gatherings, music hall singalongs and church hymning are much in evidence in the burgeoning world of the amateur choir in the 21st century. And yet even here, the pro/am barrier remains largely intact, with paid experts coming in to deliver their programme with, beyond the minimum necessary, little reference or relationship to the group with whom they perform. This is where the [Gospel Oak Chorale](https://musicintheflesh.org/about#gospel-oak-chorale) has chosen to set out its stall: a London choir of amateur, student and young professional singers together with associated collaborators, taking the embodied approach of Music in the Flesh to wider audiences and performers. This is done in the conviction that individual lives and relationships become more fulfilling if the opportunity to engage with the renewal and reinterpretation of long-standing music-making traditions is made as accessible as possible. The voice as an onboard and universally distributed instrument comes first, but others follow.

In establishing this experiment – now in its second year of monthly rehearsal and quarterly (free, [benefit](https://www.lcrf.org.uk/)) performances – we are working to link these three worlds of education, the profession and amateur music making. We have secured small grants to enable us to offer honoraria to student performers, giving them the opportunity to be a part of our community choir as members rather than visiting experts, which in turn allows us to maintain a standard of performance sufficient to broaden our intake of amateur, community-based singers. At the same time we actively seek to question, disassemble and remake many of the performance conventions associated with this kind of music, re-incorporating the centrality of the whole body in both the production and perception of sound. Singers might, for instance, move about the space at will, adapting their sound to whichever voices they happen to be passing, an experience that singer and listener share, while eye and physical contact between singers helps to attune the sound. We envisage that this kind of approach will then also inform the practice of those of our student members who head into education, professional music making or neither, so that the experience of a choir in which professional and amateur are not fenced apart goes with them into the classrooms, ensembles, concert halls and walks of life where they end up.

The experiment is small, and its results will lie in the quality of what participants go on to do, not in meeting indicators set in advance. We aim, in a small way, to show that humanistic education does more, and reaches further, when it reaches more humans.
